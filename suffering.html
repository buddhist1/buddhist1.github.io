Buddha's prescription to end suffering

The core of Buddhism is 4 noble truth, the simplified version is below:

  1. The Truth of Suffering: Life inherently involves dissatisfaction, pain, and the fundamental impermanence of all things.
  2. The Truth of the Origin: Suffering is caused by craving, attachment, and a misguided desire for things to be different than they are.
  3. The Truth of Cessation: Suffering can be overcome by letting go of these cravings and achieving a state of mental liberation.
  4. The Truth of the Path: The way to end suffering is to follow a balanced life of ethical conduct, mental discipline, and wisdom.

note: Buddha never said that 'life IS suffering', but rather mentioned that life contains/involves suffering to some degree. Following dhamma aims to reduce this suffering

In this blog, I will focus on the second noble truth that is widely misunderstood. Suffering (dukkha) is caused by lobha (attachment), dosa (aversion) and moha (ignorance)

A very simple example that I'm sure almost everyone has experienced before, is bad exam result. Let say student A studied all night and got a 'C' on his test, while student B totally forgot to study and also got a 'C'. Both of them pass, but student A is furious because he had put so much effort into the test and got a bad result. However, student B is absolutely delighted because he passed even though he totally forgot to study.

This proves that suffering is inherently generated from the mind and perspective. The same external environment, getting a 'C' on a test, can result in 2 totally opposite emotion. This suffering comes from the attachment of student A because thinks that he deserves a good grade simply because he studied hard. In reality, a lot of things in life is out of our control.

The second example is getting sick vs muscle soreness from exercise. When you are having a cold, sometimes it presents as muscle soreness all over the body. Everyone can universally agree that this is an unpleasant sensation. When you had an intense exercise, it can also cause muscle soreness the next day. Sometimes more severe in terms of physical pain compared to when you are sick. However, it feels weirdly pleasant because you knew that you had a good workout the previous day.

In Buddha's theory, this is due to dosa / aversion. The physical pain is similar, but the mental distress from sickness is due to aversion, the subtle 'I want to this sickness to stop' vs 'I had a good workout' in the back of your mind. Being physically tired/low energy after going around the city on a vacation is also similar to the tiredness due to feeling sick, but former results in satisfaction while the latter feels terrible.

A more concrete example is chronic pain. Let say that there are 2 people with different degree of chronic pain. Person A's pain scale is 2/10, Person B's pain scale is 5/10. Person A is suffering mentally because he is in low grade constant pain. However, all of sudden we found a medication that can reduce the Person B's pain scale to 2/10. Person B who has been suffering for years, feels extremely happy because his pain is now 2/10, the best day of his life. Now, Both live in the same pain scale, but one is suffering and while the other is extremely happy. You can also observe this in your life. Someone who has difficulty accepting the hardship of life (high aversion) will mentally suffer more intensely that someone who doesn't.

The third part is moha (ignorance). This has a broad definition of not understanding dhamma / misconception of reality. E.g. attachment to things that is fundamentally impermanent, rebirth in lower realms due to bad deed, doesn't understand the law of karma, etc.

All mental suffering, no matter how subtle, can be traced back to one of these 3; Either attachment, aversion, or ignorance.

IMPORTANT

A lot of people think that the solution to this is 'just stop attachment/stop wanting things' which is flawed. Buddha has never said 'just stop wanting things', but he provides frameworks for people to follow e.g:

  • following noble 8 fold path
  • practicing dana, sila, samadhi, panna
  • understanding anicca, dukkha, anatta
  • understanding dependent origination, law of karma, etc

Also, not all desire/wanting is a bad thing. Buddha classified desires as wholesome (chanda) and unwholesome (tanha). Former leads to less suffering while the latter leads to more suffering.

Buddha also taught things differently to monks vs lay people. For monks, he frequently warns against the danger of sensual pleasures. For lay people, he frequently taught to live life ethically, skillfully and allow enjoying sensual pleasures with wisdom (source 1, source 2). Monks aim to achieve nibbana in this life while lay people aims to achieve at least stream entry (sotapanna).

A sotapanna is someone who has successfully eliminated 3 lower fetters. Because of this, nibbana is guaranteed in 7 rebirths max (source). Below stream enterer, there is actually dhammanusari (follower by wisdom) and saddhanusari (follower by faith). Both of these people are also guaranteed stream entry before death and incapable to do anything that would lead to lower rebirth (source).
