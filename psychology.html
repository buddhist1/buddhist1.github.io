Buddhism philosophy in modern therapy

CBT is the "gold standard" of modern brief therapy. it operates on the principle that our thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are interconnected. If you change a distorted thought, the emotional response and resulting behavior will also shift.

CBT works this way:

  1. work to identify "Automatic Negative Thoughts" (ANTs) that arise in stressful moments. This involves looking for patterns like catastrophizing (expecting the worst) or mind-reading (assuming you know what others think)

  2. Cognitive Restructuring: Once a distorted thought is caught, you treat it as a "hypothesis" rather than a fact. You look for objective evidence for and against that thought to develop a more balanced perspective

  3. Behavioral Experiments: You test new responses in the real world. For example, if you fear a specific social interaction, you might purposefully engage in a small version of it to see if your feared outcome actually happens

CBT doesn't aim to "think positive" in a way that ignores reality; rather, it aims to challenge the negative thoughts by presenting evidence and trying to prove your thoughts are wrong

Another equally effective method is ACT (Acceptance & Commitment Therapy), which has interesting overlap with Buddhist concept. ACT works through 'Cognitive Defusion' where one trains to see thoughts as just thoughts, a transient, rising and passing phenomenon. In a traditional "positive thinking" approach, you might try to tell yourself, "I am confident" or "People will like me." In ACT, the goal isn't to get rid of the anxiety, but to change your relationship with it so it doesn't stop you from doing what matters.

For example, if you have the following thought: "I am incompetent"

ACT deals with this by seeing thoughts simply as a mental phenomenon.

  • Fusion: "I am incompetent." (This feels like a cold, hard fact.)
  • Step 1: "I’m having the thought that I am incompetent." (This acknowledges it is a mental event.)
  • Step 2: "I notice I’m having the thought that I am incompetent." (This places you as the observer of the process.)

In Buddhism, there are multiple suttas explaining similar concept. One of them is Mahāsatipaṭṭhānasutta (source)

And so they meditate observing an aspect of the mind internally, externally, and both internally and externally. They meditate observing the mind as liable to originate, as liable to fall, and as liable to both originate and vanish. Or mindfulness is established that the mind exists, to the extent necessary for knowledge and mindfulness. They meditate independent, not grasping at anything in the world. That’s how a mendicant meditates by observing an aspect of the mind
