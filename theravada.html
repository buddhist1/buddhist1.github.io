History of Theravada

Theravada, by definition, translates to "The School of the Elders" because it is the oldest surviving branch of Buddhism.

Theravada school traces its lineage back to the First Buddhist Council, held shortly after the Buddha’s death. During this council, a group of senior monks (the "Elders") gathered to recite and agree upon the authentic teachings and monastic rules (Vinaya) to ensure they remained unchanged.

For approximately 400 to 500 years after the Buddha’s death, the Pali Canon existed only in the minds and voices of the monastic community. Monks organized themselves into specialized groups called Bhanakas. Different groups were responsible for memorizing specific sections of the teachings (e.g., one group focused on the Digha Nikaya or "Long Discourses"). Monks did not recite alone. They chanted in groups. This acted as a built-in error correction mechanism. If one monk misremembered a word or stumbled, the collective voice of the group would carry the correct version, instantly correcting the individual.

Periodically, a council were held where thousands of monks would cross-reference their memorized versions. If any discrepancies were found, they were debated and settled by the "Elders" to maintain a single, authorized version.

Why Oral Preservation is Often Considered Superior to Text

  1. Protection Against Forgery and Interpolation It is surprisingly easy to sneak a new sentence into a physical manuscript or change a word during a silent transcription. It is much harder to convince an entire community of 500 monks to simultaneously change a verse they have been chanting together every day for decades. The social synchronization of oral chanting acted as a "blockchain" of its time.

  2. Immunity to Physical Decay In the humid climates of South and Southeast Asia, physical materials like palm leaves, bark, or animal skins rot, burn, or are eaten by insects. A book can be lost or destroyed in a single fire. However, a living tradition stored in the minds of thousands of people across different regions is nearly impossible to "delete."

The Transition to Text

It wasn't until the Fourth Buddhist Council in Sri Lanka (around 29 BCE) that the teachings were finally written down on palm leaves. This was done primarily because of the threat of famine and war, which risked killing off enough monks to break the "living chain" of the oral tradition.

Realism

In EBTs (early buddhist texts), it does not shy away from the Buddha’s humanity. They record him getting backaches in old age, suffering from indigestion, and eventually dying a physical death. A central tenet of the Pali Canon is Ehipassiko, which translates to "come and see for yourself." The teachings encourage followers to test the doctrine against their own experience rather than accepting it on blind faith. This empirical approach aligns closely with the scientific method. Suttas also put heavy emphasis on personal accountability e.g. your happiness and liberation is totally under your control, not under the mercy of other deities. In a lot of suttas where Buddha interacted with lay people, he usually approach lay people's question with logical reasoning

Authenticity

Due to debates and discrepancies throughout the history of Buddhist councils, the community eventually fractured into several distinct lineages. Over time, these differences evolved beyond simple rules into deep philosophical shifts. There are estimated ~20-30 different schools today. However, Theravada is considered the oldest lineage for the following reasons:

  1. Early Codification: The Pali Canon was committed to writing in the 1st century BCE (the Fourth Council in Sri Lanka). Compared to other lineages, which appeared centuries later, these texts are generally considered by historians to contain the "earliest layer" of Buddhist doctrine.

  2. Philological Analysis: Linguistic studies of the Pali language provides strong evidence for the authenticity of the teaching. Pali is a Middle Indo-Aryan dialect that, while not identical to the Magadhi language the Buddha likely spoke, is closely related to the vernaculars of North India during that era. When comparing the Pali Suttas to the Chinese Agamas (which were translated from lost Sanskrit versions of other early schools), the core doctrines remain remarkably consistent. This suggests that it shared a "Common Core" of teachings that existed before the first major schisms.
